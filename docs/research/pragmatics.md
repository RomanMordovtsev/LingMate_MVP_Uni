## Principles for LingMate

### 1. Pragmatics = choices in context, on both sides
- Crystal (1997), Taguchi & Roever (2017), Savić et al. (2025): pragmatics studies the speaker's choices, the constraints on them, and their effect on the hearer. For an L2 user one constraint is the developing grammar ("Please I want the book" vs. a mitigated request).
- Leech (1983): **pragmalinguistics** (which forms do the act) vs. **sociopragmatics** (which social factors make a form appropriate).
- **Design rule:** the tutor checks two things, not one: is the sentence correct, and is it right for *this* person and situation. A sentence can be grammatical and still "wrong".

### 2. Speech acts: the same words, different acts
- Austin (1962): locution / illocution / perlocution; Searle (1976) classes (assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, declarations); Searle (1975): direct vs. indirect speech acts ("Can you pass the salt?", "It's cold in here").
- Class exercises: literal answers to indirect acts ("Did you put your jacket on?" — "No", and leaves) are a typical learner failure.
- **Design rule:** role-plays include indirect acts the learner must read correctly (a landlord's hint, a waiter's "¿Algo más?"), and the tutor checks the learner's *reply* to the act, not only its grammar.

### 3. Politeness: face, distance, power, imposition
- Brown & Levinson (1987): positive face (to be liked) vs. negative face (not to be imposed on); face-threatening acts (FTAs); strategies: bald on record → positive politeness → negative politeness → off record → don't do the act. Choice depends on social distance (D), power (P) and rank of imposition (R).
- LoCastro (2012): the model is Western-centric and leaves out non-verbal politeness; later work adds face-*enhancing* acts (Kerbrat-Orecchioni 1997).
- **Design rule:** each scenario stores D, P and R (friend / neighbour / bank clerk / doctor; small vs. big favour). The same task at different D/P/R values gives a natural "pragmatic variation" drill: ask a friend vs. ask the landlord for the same thing.

### 4. Speech acts have a known structure — teach the parts
- **Requests** (Trosborg 1995; Alcón et al. 2005): head act (direct → conventionally indirect hearer-based → speaker-based → hint) + internal modifiers (softeners like "un momento", "por casualidad"; openers) + external modifiers (preparator, grounder, disarmer, "please"). Learners underuse internal softeners and sometimes over-explain with grounders (verbosity; House & Kasper 1987).
- **Refusals** (Beebe et al. 1990): pre-refusal → head act (often indirect: regret, excuse, alternative, postponement) → post-refusal; adjuncts such as positive opinion and thanks. Called "a major cross-cultural sticking point".
- **Apologies** (Olshtain & Cohen 1983): expression of apology, account, acknowledgement of responsibility, offer of repair, promise of forbearance; the sense of how severe an offence is differs across cultures (Kondo 1997).
- **Suggestions** (Martínez-Flor 2005): direct / conventionalised ("¿Por qué no…?", "Si yo fuera tú…") / indirect (impersonal, hints). **Advice** (Hinkel 1994; Houck & Fujimori 2010): direct / softened / indirect; unsolicited advice reads very differently across cultures.
- **Compliments and responses** (Ishihara 2010) and **disagreement** (Malamed 2010): token agreement before disagreeing ("Sí, pero…"), hedges, delay devices (Pomerantz 1984).
- **Design rule:** for every speech act we teach, the unit stores a small strategy set (head-act types + 3–5 useful modifiers) in Spanish, not one fixed phrase. Feedback names the missing part ("you refused without a reason or thanks") rather than just rewriting the sentence.

### 5. Pragmatics is teachable; explicit instruction usually wins
- Reviews in Usó-Juan (2010): requests and their modifiers are teachable; explicit instruction (metapragmatic explanation + awareness tasks) generally beats implicit-only, but recasts also help (Fukuya & Zhang 2002); short treatments fade on delayed tests (Salazar 2003); low-proficiency learners gained less (Codina 2008).
- Conditions for pragmatic development (Kasper 2001): appropriate input, collaborative practice, metapragmatic reflection.
- Teaching sequence used across the chapters (Martínez-Flor & Usó-Juan 2006, "6Rs"): explore the act in the L1 → explore it in the L2 from real or film data → recognise D/P/R effects → produce in role-plays → feedback and reflection.
- **Design rule:** a "speech-act lesson" in the lesson engine follows that order: (1) one question about how the learner would do it in Russian/English; (2) two short Spanish dialogues with different D/P/R; (3) a ranking task ("which is most appropriate here?") — auto-checked, no LLM; (4) role-play; (5) feedback on strategy + form; (6) review item in SRS. Spread practice over several days, because single short treatments fade.

### 6. L1 pragmatic transfer is real — and the learner may choose not to converge
- Transfer studies (Beebe et al. 1990; Maeshiba et al. 1996; Al-Issa 2003): learners carry over directness, excuse content and status sensitivity from the L1.
- Kondo (2010), Ishihara (2010), Kasper (1997): aim for *optimal* rather than total convergence; learners may resist native-speaker norms on purpose (subjectivity, identity). Ishihara's assessment lets the learner state the intended effect ("I want to sound like most people / more formal / more friendly").
- **Design rule:** extend transfer maps (1.5) with a pragmatic layer for RU→ES (e.g. directness of requests, use of diminutives and "¿me pones…?" in service talk, refusals with thanks + reason). Treat these as hypotheses until checked with Roman or a corpus. Pragmatic feedback states the likely effect on a Spanish hearer and offers options; it never tells the learner who to be.

### 7. Culture and interculture
- Class sheet on culture (concrete expressions → behaviours → explicit beliefs → deeply held beliefs: time, space, hierarchy, high/low context). Spain examples from class: personal distance, birthday ear-pulling, a shop assistant chatting with another customer, "No ha llegado su dinero todavía" at the bank.
- **Design rule:** intercultural notes (4.9) follow these layers and end with one reflection question ("What would happen in your country?"), never a stereotype stated as fact.

### 8. Assessment of pragmatics
- Ishihara (2010) rubric: directness/politeness/tone, choice of strategy, comprehensibility of intention — each 1–4. Class criteria for a speech-act lesson: oral and written input in social situations, real-use patterns, a number of patterns that can be learned in one lesson, fit to level.
- **Design rule:** the eval harness gets a small pragmatic rubric (strategy present? register fits D/P/R? intention clear?) next to grammar accuracy; patterns per lesson are capped (3–5) and matched to CEFR level.

## Course caution
The course grade is a lesson the student designs on a speech act (75%). LingMate may use these principles, but the agent never designs Roman's assessed lesson or writes text for it.
