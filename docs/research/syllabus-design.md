## Principles for LingMate

### 1. Backward design from real needs
- Richards (2013): forward, central and backward design; CEFR planning starts from learners' real-life communicative needs and aligns curriculum, teaching and assessment.
- Van den Branden (2016): TBLT starts with a needs analysis.
- **Design rule:** the curriculum is generated from the learner's profile (goal, situation, place, interests) → target real-world tasks → CEFR descriptors → scenarios → assessment of those same tasks.

### 2. Analytic (task-based) syllabus as the backbone, linguistic syllabus as a layer
- Robinson (2009): synthetic syllabi (grammar items in sequence) vs analytic syllabi (holistic language use); analytic ones respect SLA processes and learner differences.
- Ellis & Shintani (2014): grammatical syllabuses do not reflect how acquisition happens; lexical/formulaic sequences fit better; a linguistic syllabus works best as a supplement to a task syllabus.
- **Design rule:** the unit of the course is a *task/scenario*. Grammar topics form a secondary map (for Spanish: Plan Curricular del Instituto Cervantes; for English: EAQUALS Core Inventory / English Profile) used to pick focus-on-form targets and pre-task tips. Teach useful chunks whole ("¿Me podría decir…?").

### 3. Task cycle: pre-task → task → post-task
- TBLT slides; Van den Branden (2016).
- **Design rule:** every scenario has (a) pre-task: goal, 3–6 key chunks, one form tip; (b) task: the dialogue with a non-linguistic outcome; (c) post-task: feedback summary, highlighted forms, items to the review queue, optional repeat later.
- Task complexity grows along: number of elements, predictability, time pressure, here-and-now vs there-and-then.

### 4. Interaction quality (Loewen 2025)
- Referential questions (genuine, the asker doesn't know the answer) beat display questions.
- Explicit and output-prompting feedback is on average more effective than implicit recasts; timing (immediate vs delayed) is a design choice.
- **Design rule:** the tutor asks real questions about the learner's life; corrections follow morphosyntax.md §2; offer an "end-of-scenario feedback" mode for learners who prefer fluency-first.

### 5. Counterbalanced instruction (Lyster 2017)
- Content/meaning alone is not enough: noticing → awareness → practice with feedback. Prompts beat recasts for forms the learner already knows.
- **Design rule:** same as morphosyntax.md §2 and §10.

### 6. Scaffolding (sociocultural, ZPD)
- **Design rule:** graded hints — first a nudge, then a partial cue, then the full answer.

### 7. Projects and content (PBL, CLIL)
- Stoller (2002): project work = content focus, autonomy, collaboration, integrated skills, final product, 10-step sequence. PBL slides: learner-negotiated topics drawn from personal experience.
- CLIL / CBI (Dalton-Puffer; Llinares & Morton): learn content through the language.
- **Design rule:** multi-day mini-projects with a real product (e.g. "write to your landlord", "plan a weekend trip", "prepare for a doctor's visit"); a content mode for learners without a relocation goal (discuss articles/topics they care about in the target language).

### 8. CEFR can-do descriptors for progress and assessment
- CEFR Companion Volume (2020): illustrative descriptors A1–C2, incl. *Online conversation and discussion* and mediation scales. Can-do perspective, not deficit.
- **Design rule:** each scenario maps to 1–3 descriptors; progress screen shows descriptors with evidence from completed tasks.
